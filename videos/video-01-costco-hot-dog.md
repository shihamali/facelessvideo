**Description (first two lines):**
Costco has sold its hot dog and soda for $1.50 since 1985 and loses money on it. Here is why that is the smartest decision in retail, and where Costco's profit really comes from.

## Script

### Section 0: Hook (0:00–0:20)

**VO:**
This hot dog and soda cost a dollar fifty in 1985 [F1]. Today it costs a dollar fifty. If the price had simply kept up with inflation, it would be more than four dollars [F2]. Costco loses money on it, and the company has no plan to change that. The reason tells you where its profit really comes from, and it is not from the things on its shelves.

**Visuals:** Slow push-in on a hot dog. A price tag reads $1.50 while a year counter spins from 1985 to today. A ghost price climbs past $4 beside it, then gets crossed out.

### Section 1: The threat (0:20–1:30)

**VO:**
Years ago, Costco's chief executive, Craig Jelinek, went to the company's co-founder, Jim Sinegal, with a problem. They were losing money on the hot dog. He wanted to raise the price.

As Jelinek later told the story, Sinegal's answer was short. "If you raise the effing hot dog, I will kill you. Figure it out." [F3]

So they figured it out. Costco stopped buying hot dogs from an outside supplier and started making its own, under its Kirkland Signature brand, in its own plant in California [F4]. Owning the factory was cheaper than raising the price.

It has done the same thing elsewhere. When the cost of soda rose, Costco swapped its fountain supplier rather than touch the price [F5]. And to keep its rotisserie chicken at four ninety-nine, where it has sat since 2009, it built an entire poultry operation in Nebraska, at a cost of hundreds of millions of dollars [F6].

A company does not go to that much trouble for a snack. It does it because the snack is doing a different job.

**Visuals:** Archival stills of Sinegal and Jelinek. The quote appears word by word on a black screen with the music out. Factory line footage. Map pin drops on California, then Nebraska. Rotisserie chickens turning with a "$4.99 since 2009" tag.

### Section 2: The loss leader (1:30–3:10)

**VO:**
Retailers have a name for this: a loss leader. You sell one thing at a loss to get people through the door, then make it back on everything else. Supermarkets do it with milk and eggs.

Costco's version is different, because the hot dog is at the exit. You have already shopped by the time you reach it.

So it is not there to pull you in. It is there to shape the last thing you feel before you leave. You have just spent three hundred dollars, and on the way out the company hands you lunch for a dollar fifty. The feeling you take to the car park is that this place is on your side.

That feeling has a number attached. Costco sells well over a hundred million of these combos a year [F7]. Each one is a small, repeated proof of a single claim: we will not rip you off.

And that claim matters more to Costco than to almost any other shop, because of what it is really selling.

**Visuals:** Store floor plan animation with a shopper dot moving from entrance to checkout to food court at the exit. Receipt scrolling to a $300 total, then a $1.50 receipt beside it. Counter ticking past 100,000,000.

*— Mid-roll slot 1 —*

### Section 3: The real product (3:10–5:20)

**VO:**
Here is the part most shoppers never see.

A normal supermarket marks its products up by a quarter or more [F8]. Costco limits itself to about fourteen percent on brand names and fifteen on its own Kirkland products [F9]. After paying staff, rent and shipping, there is very little left. On the goods themselves, Costco comes close to breaking even.

So where is the profit?

It is in the card in your wallet. To shop at Costco you pay a membership fee: sixty-five dollars a year, or a hundred and thirty for the executive tier [F10]. Those fees bring in around five billion dollars a year [F11]. And unlike a television or a tray of muffins, a membership costs Costco almost nothing to supply. Nearly all of it is profit.

Put that next to the company's total profit and the picture is clear. Membership fees are equal to well over half of everything Costco earns [F11].

In other words, Costco is not really a shop that charges a fee. It is a membership business that runs a shop. The warehouse exists to make the membership worth renewing.

And people renew. In the United States and Canada, about nine in ten members sign up again every year [F12].

Now the hot dog makes sense. If Costco made fifty cents more on each one, it would earn tens of millions of dollars. But the fees are worth billions, and they depend entirely on members believing the prices are honest. Trading that belief for hot dog money would be a terrible deal.

**Visuals:** Side-by-side bars: supermarket markup versus Costco markup. A dollar bill sliced into cost of goods, wages, rent, with a sliver left. Membership card flips into frame; a stack labelled "fees" rises next to a stack labelled "total profit". Simple loop diagram: low prices → trust → renewal → fees → low prices.

### Section 4: Why the prices can be that low (5:20–7:20)

**VO:**
The fees explain why Costco does not need big markups. They do not explain how it gets prices low enough to begin with. That comes from a choice that looks, at first, like a weakness.

A typical supermarket carries more than thirty thousand different products. Costco carries about four thousand [F13].

There is one kind of ketchup. Perhaps two kinds of olive oil. You do not get to choose much.

But think about it from the supplier's side. At a supermarket, a ketchup maker competes with nine others for a slice of the shelf. At Costco, the winner gets the whole shelf, in every warehouse, in bulk. That is an enormous order, and Costco uses it to demand an enormous discount.

Fewer products also means fewer things to stock, track and throw away. Goods arrive on pallets and are sold from the same pallets. Nobody stacks shelves by hand.

And when a supplier will not give a good enough price, Costco does what it did with the hot dog. It makes its own. Kirkland Signature now sells tens of billions of dollars of goods a year, more than many famous brands [F14].

**Visuals:** A supermarket aisle with dozens of ketchup brands dissolves into a single pallet of one. Counter: 30,000 vs 4,000. Forklift footage. Kirkland products lining up in a row.

*— Mid-roll slot 2 —*

### Section 5: The limits (7:20–8:40)

**VO:**
None of this is charity, and it does not work for everyone.

The model depends on you buying in bulk, which means it suits households with storage space, a car and money to spend up front. Buying a year of olive oil is only a bargain if you can afford it today.

It also works on you in the other direction. Costco moves products around the warehouse on purpose, so you walk past things you did not come for. The low prices are real. So is the three hundred dollar receipt.

And the fee does rise. It went up in 2024, for the first time in seven years [F10]. Costco is careful with those increases for the same reason it protects the hot dog: the whole machine runs on members feeling they are getting the better half of the deal.

**Visuals:** Overloaded trolley. Pantry shelves stacked with bulk goods. Fee timeline with step-ups. Shopper wandering past televisions on the way to the milk.

### Section 6: Payoff and call to action (8:40–9:30)

**VO:**
So the hot dog is not a product. It is an advert, and probably the cheapest one Costco runs. It costs the company a little on every sale, and it buys the one thing its profit depends on, which is your belief that the price is fair.

When a new finance chief took over in 2024, one of the first things he was asked was whether the price would change. He said the dollar fifty hot dog was safe [F15].

Costco hides its real business behind a hot dog. Airlines hide theirs behind a seat. The big American airlines now make so much from selling miles to banks that the flying can look like a side business. That story is on screen now.

And if you want to keep seeing where the money really goes, subscribe.

**Visuals:** Return to the opening hot dog shot, price tag still $1.50. End screen: next-video card (Video 2, airlines) on the left, subscribe button on the right, 20 seconds.
